Write a seminar slide deck for this econometrics working paper — a 15–20 minute conference-style pitch talk. The PRIMARY quality measure is clarity: an econometrician outside this subfield must be able to follow every slide on first read and leave understanding what the paper establishes and why it matters. Prefer plain language over notation; introduce a symbol only when a later slide needs it.

OUTPUT FORMAT — plain markdown, nothing else (no code fences, no JSON, no preamble):
- `---` on its own line separates slides.
- The FIRST slide opens with `# <talk title>` (Title Case); every later slide opens with `## <slide title>` (sentence case — see the title rule below).
- To show a formal statement, write the single line `@formal <obj_id>` where the statement should appear — its audited body is injected verbatim at render time. NEVER write displayed math yourself (no `$$…$$`, `\[…\]`, or align environments). Inline math like \(\tau_n\) with paper notation is allowed in prose.
- To restate a result in plain language, write `@informal <obj_id>: <one-sentence headline>` — it renders labeled as informal, linked to the audited statement. The headline must not claim more than the formal body: when the body proves a bound or inequality, say "at most"/"at least", never an exact rate or value, and keep any hypothesis hedge that changes the claim's strength.
- Optionally, up to 2 SCHEMATIC figures where a picture genuinely clarifies structure: write the single line `@figure <kebab-name>: <caption saying exactly what the diagram shows>`. Good schematics: a DAG, a regime comparison, an estimator pipeline. The renderer draws ONLY labeled boxes connected by arrows — the caption must describe a box-and-arrow schematic, never an axis, grid, curve, or plot it cannot draw. Never data plots, numeric curves, or invented quantities — the figure is drawn from the caption and its slide's content alone and renders labeled "illustrative".
- Everything else is ordinary markdown: short bullets and short sentences. No paragraph walls; at most ~6 bullets or ~10 short lines per slide.

DECK SHAPE (11–17 slides, ~1–1.5 minutes each). A seminar talk follows the arc below, but the arc is a storyline, not a form to fill in: merge, reorder, or drop a beat when the paper's own logic reads better, and never announce the arc in prose — the audience should experience the structure, not be told about it. Slide titles follow the title rule below.
1. Title slide: talk title (may differ from the paper title if a plainer phrasing is clearer), then the TL;DR line as a one-sentence pitch.
2. The result up front (1 slide): what the paper establishes and why it matters — the audience knows the destination before any setup.
3. Motivation (1–2 slides): why anyone should care, in concrete terms; introduce a running example if the paper admits one.
4. Setup (2–3 slides): the minimal cast of notation, each key assumption stated in plain words; use `@formal` for the load-bearing ones only.
5. The central insight (1 slide, when the paper rests on one): that insight alone, in plain words, before any machinery.
6. What was known (1 slide, or woven into motivation and results where the contrast lands harder): from the related-work brief — the state of the art and the gap this paper fills.
7. Main results (2–4 slides): for each main theorem, an `@informal` headline first, then `@formal` on the same or next slide. Where the novel construction appears, its advantage over the naive approach belongs inside the result's own story (INTUITION register below), never on a separate commentary slide.
8. Why it is true (1–3 slides): the essential proof ideas in words. A multi-step argument (e.g. a lower-bound construction) is told as a short story — one step per slide or per bullet run, never one compressed list. No proof steps, no displayed math.
9. Close (1 slide): the contributions restated affirmatively; open directions only if the paper states them.

RULES:
- Every theorem in the formal-statement catalog below must appear on some slide via `@formal` or `@informal`. Definitions and assumptions appear only where the talk needs them.
- `@formal` is reserved for statements the audience must see exactly: the main theorems, and whichever assumptions must be shown to understand the paper (all of them if they are all load-bearing; standard bookkeeping assumptions are narrated instead). NEVER inject definition blocks — constructions and estimators are described in words, at the two registers below.
- Describe the paper's machinery at two registers. MECHANISM register — what is split, thresholded, compared, or averaged, and at which symbolic scale — one plain spoken sentence per component; every standard ingredient gets this register only, one line at most. INTUITION register — why the design works — is reserved for the paper's central novel contribution (a new estimator/construction, bound argument, or identification idea): say what the naive approach (plug-in, standard estimator, direct bound) achieves, exactly where and why it breaks, and how the novel piece repairs that failure — when the gain is quantitative, name the error term removed or shrunk at the paper's own symbolic scales. A listener should leave able to re-derive the novelty's logic, not just recite its parts.
- Talk altitude, not paper altitude: a slide carries the idea; drop indexing conventions, measurability/clipping caveats, and generality bookkeeping from prose unless the point of the slide is exactly that. One plain mechanism sentence beats a displayed formula.
- Use only obj_ids from the catalog. Do not invent statements, numbers, or claims not present in the inputs. State a symbolic scale or rate only by copying it verbatim from a catalog body or the outline's notation — never simplify, truncate, or recombine scales from memory.
- Prose may call out a diagram only when the same slide carries the matching `@figure`.
- Never open a claim with what the paper does NOT do; scope conditions are stated affirmatively ("under bounded degree…", "for fixed \(\beta\)…").
- Titles, in preference order. DEFAULT: a sentence-case message title — a short assertion or question a listener could repeat afterwards, stating the slide's content, never the talk's own structure as a sentence ("the destination/takeaway of this talk is…"). ACCEPTABLE ALTERNATIVE where it fits the slide's role: a conventional seminar heading — Overview, Outline, Motivation, Research question, Background, Setup, Model, Notation, Assumptions, Related literature, Contributions, Main results, Key idea, Intuition, Proof sketch, Example, Extensions, Discussion, Limitations, Takeaways, Conclusion, Open questions, Future work, or another heading in standard seminar use — or a plain inventory label (an assumptions list, the "Also in the paper" slide). NEVER: a coined or talk-craft phrase as a heading ("Punchline", "The Novelty").
- Show, don't announce: no self-praise vocabulary on slides ("punchline", "novelty", "our novel…") and no meta-narration ("we now turn to…"). Newness is conveyed by the contrast itself — what was known, what this establishes — never by declaring it new.
- Write for the ear: each bullet a single self-contained sentence or fragment a speaker could say aloud.
- Define every coined term at first use: a dichotomy or named construct the paper introduces (heavy/light, crossed, clipped, …) gets one plain line saying its criterion or meaning before the deck relies on it.
- One idea per slide: if a slide makes two points, split it — prefer more, lighter slides within the cap.
- Pose the question before the answer: where natural, end a slide with the question the next slide answers ("what is the best possible error?"), so the deck reads as inquiry, not inventory.
- Carry the running example through the talk: after introducing it, interpret at least one assumption and one main result in its terms.
- "Why it is true" slides carry no `@formal` blocks and never reference internal or synthesized helper objects — plain words only.
- When the catalog contains variant/predecessor theorems subsumed by results already shown, cover them together on ONE compact "Also in the paper" slide of plain one-line `@informal` headlines. Never narrate the formalization or drafting process itself.
