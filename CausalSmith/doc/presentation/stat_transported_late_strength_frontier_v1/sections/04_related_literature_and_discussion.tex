\section{Related Literature and Discussion}

The paper belongs first to the LATE and principal-stratification literature. The source experiment uses the encouragement-design logic of \citet{Bloom1984}, \citet{ImbensAngrist1994}, and \citet{AngristImbensRubin1996}: conditional randomization, exclusion, and monotonicity turn the source reduced form and first stage into a complier contrast. Principal stratification supplies the population-level interpretation of compliance types and target complier effects \citep{BalkePearl1997,ImbensRubin1997,FrangakisRubin2002,ImbensRosenbaum2005,HeckmanVytlacil2005}. In the present setting, \cref{obj:prop:compact-causal-range} identifies the transported reduced form and transported first stage through weighted source contrasts and places the target complier contrast inside the compact parameter space \(\Theta=[-1,1]\).

The weak-identification component follows the robust ratio-inference tradition. Anderson--Rubin and Fieller procedures invert reduced-form score restrictions, giving ratio inference through the score restriction itself \citep{AndersonRubin1949,Fieller1954}. Subsequent work characterizes the consequences of weak instruments for distributional approximation, confidence sets, and expected length \citep{GleserHwang1987,Dufour1997,BoundJaegerBaker1995,StaigerStock1997,StockWrightYogo2002,Moreira2003,AndrewsMoreiraStock2006,Mikusheva2010,AndrewsStockSun2019,BertanhaMoreira2020,Ma2023,SmuclerLanniMasip2025}. The oracle score construction in \cref{obj:def:inversion-handle} is the transported analogue of this logic: it tests candidate values by centering the weighted reduced form after subtracting the candidate times the weighted first stage. \Cref{obj:thm:oracle-converse,obj:thm:oracle-score-inversion-attainment} then characterize the minimax expected-length order for the transported ratio as \(\min\{1,t_0^{-1/2}\}\).

Transportability and generalizability enter through the target-to-source covariate weighting structure. Work on transporting and generalizing treatment effects emphasizes support, covariate reweighting, and target-population interpretation \citep{HotzImbensMortimer2005,PearlBareinboim2011,BareinboimPearl2013,BareinboimPearl2016,ColeStuart2010,StuartColeBradshawLeaf2011,Tipton2013,Dahabreh2020,DahabrehPetitoRobertson2020,Nguyen2017}. Here the transported-IV restrictions in \cref{obj:def:model-class} combine those support and contrast conditions with an IV compliance structure. The effective-strength scalar \(t_n=n\mu_n^2/\kappa_n\) summarizes the transported first-stage mean \(\mu_n\) and Kish dispersion \(\kappa_n\), so the expected-length comparison uses a single scale for weak compliance and covariate reweighting.

The feasible procedures connect to semiparametric weighting and modern transported noncompliance methods. Inverse-probability weighting, augmented estimation, and sample-splitting ideas motivate estimating transported nuisance components while retaining valid target-population interpretation \citep{HiranoImbensRidder2003,RobinsRotnitzkyZhao1994,VanDerLaanRubin2006,Chernozhukov2018}.

\paragraph{Transported noncompliance.}
\Citet{ChenHuang2025}, cited as an arXiv working paper, identify and estimate transported CACE in a two-sample generalizability design with source noncompliance and target population information; their inferential object is transported-CACE identification and estimation. \Citet{RudolphVanderLaan2017}, a journal article, develop robust estimators for encouragement-design effects transported across sites. \Citet{Ross2026}, also a journal article, study trial transport when participation changes adherence, with transported trial effects as the inferential target. The present paper adds the honest minimax expected-length frontier for a compact transported-CACE ratio under a triangular-array weak first stage and Kish weight dispersion, together with finite-cell procedures that attain the oracle order.

\paragraph{Weak-IV and two-sample IV inference.}
\Citet{Ma2023}, cited as an arXiv working paper, gives identification-robust LATE inference with high-dimensional covariates. \Citet{SmuclerLanniMasip2025}, also cited as an arXiv working paper, studies the properties of LATE confidence sets obtained by score-test inversion. \Citet{ChoiGuShen2018} and \citet{ChoiShen2019} instead give weak-instrument-robust inference for linear IV coefficients assembled from two samples. The present paper's source experiment and target covariate sample define a covariate-transport problem, and its positive scope is an honest frontier indexed by \(n\mu_n^2/\kappa_n\) with finite-cell target-weight learning.

Relative to transported-ATE efficiency and minimax benchmarks, the parameter here is a compact transported complier ratio whose denominator may shrink along the triangular array. Under the stated transported-IV, bounded-outcome, overlap, and dispersion conditions, the frontier is indexed by the ratio strength \(n\mu_n^2/\kappa_n\), whereas regular transported-ATE benchmarks use root-\(n\) target-mean scales.

Relative to classical weak-IV and two-sample IV inference, the present setting adds target-to-source covariate weighting and finite-cell target-law learning. Under the stated finite-cell and regular-cell conditions, the expected-length order records first-stage weakness and the dispersion induced by transporting the covariate distribution through the single scale \(n\mu_n^2/\kappa_n\).

Taken together, the results give a finite expected-length characterization for transported complier ratios in the stated transported-IV model classes, with the stated overlap, transport, positivity, envelope, dispersion, sample-size, and cell-growth conditions. The compact causal range in \cref{obj:prop:compact-causal-range} keeps length measured on \(\Theta\), while \cref{obj:thm:fixed-geometry-frontier} shows that the fixed-geometry minimax value is indexed by the effective-strength threshold \(t_0\). This synthesis places transported noncompliance on the same weak-ratio inference scale as classical weak-IV analysis, with \(\kappa_n\) recording the additional variation induced by moving from the source covariate law to the target covariate law.
