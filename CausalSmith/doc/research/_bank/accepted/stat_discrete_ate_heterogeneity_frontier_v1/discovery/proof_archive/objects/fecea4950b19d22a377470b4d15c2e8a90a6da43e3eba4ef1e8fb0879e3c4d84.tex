Set
\[
 a:=\frac1n,\qquad t:=\frac d{n^2},\qquad
 m:=\min\{1,u\},\qquad s:=\sigma^2.
\tag{1}
\]
Thus \(b=a+t\), the normalized selector benchmark is
\[
 r:=a+\min\{m,s+t\},
\tag{2}
\]
and the normalized converse benchmark from Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\) is
\[
 B:=b+sm.
\tag{3}
\]

First suppose \(L^2<d<nL\).  Then \(u<1\), hence \(m=u\), and
\[
 u=t\frac d{L^2}>t.
\tag{4}
\]
Put \(v:=\min(u,s)\).  Since both \(u\) and \(s+t\) are at least \(t\), and since \(s+t\ge s\),
\[
 \min(u,s+t)\ge t,
 \qquad
 \min(u,s+t)\ge v.
\tag{5}
\]
Therefore
\[
 q=a+\min(u,s+t)
 \ge a+\max(t,v)
 \ge \frac12(a+t+v)
 =\frac12\{b+\min(u,s)\}.
\tag{6}
\]
For the reverse inequality, if \(s\le u\), then \(\min(u,s+t)\le s+t=t+v\), whereas if \(u<s\), then \(\min(u,s+t)\le u=v\le t+v\).  Hence
\[
 q\le a+t+v=b+\min(u,s).
\tag{7}
\]
Equation (3) and \(m=u\) give \(\ell=b+su\), proving the first display.

We next compare the risk benchmarks on the full stipulated dimension range.  If \(\sigma\ge\underline\sigma>0\), then (3) yields
\[
 B=b+sm\ge a+\underline\sigma^2m
 \ge \min(1,\underline\sigma^2)(a+m)
 \ge \min(1,\underline\sigma^2)r.
\tag{8}
\]
The lower bound in Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\), the upper bound in Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\), and (8) give the asserted fixed-positive-radius equivalence after absorbing \(\min(1,\underline\sigma^2)\) into the lower constant.

Now fix \(C<\infty\), allowing \(s\) to range over \([0,4]\).  Each of the three stated regimes gives a comparison with constants independent of \(s\).  If \(u\ge1\), then \(m=1\) and
\[
 r=a+\min(1,s+t)\le a+s+t=B.
\tag{9}
\]
If \(u\le Cb\), then
\[
 r\le a+m\le b+Cb=(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{10}
\]
If \(s\le Cb\), then
\[
 r\le a+s+t=b+s\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{11}
\]
Combining (9)--(11) with the same two risk theorems proves the claimed uniform equivalence in each regime.

The algebraic condition \(u\le b\) is equivalent, after multiplication by \(n^2L^2>0\), to
\[
 d^2\le nL^2+dL^2.
\tag{12}
\]
The inequality \(d\le\sqrt n\,L\) implies (12) from its first right-hand term.  If \(d\le L^2\), then \(u=t(d/L^2)\le t\le b\), so the small-alphabet regime is also included.  Finally, \(s\le Cb\) is exactly the asserted radius-dominance condition.

It remains to localize possible divergence.  Consider any triangular sequence for which \(r/B\to\infty\).  Equations (9)--(11), applied for every fixed \(C\), force
\[
 u<1,\qquad \frac ub\longrightarrow\infty,\qquad
 \frac sb\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{13}
\]
Moreover, (8) shows that \(s\to0\).  Also \(u\to0\): if a subsequence had \(u\ge\eta>0\) while \(u<1\), then \(m=u\ge\eta\) and
\[
 r\le b+s\le \eta^{-1}(b+\eta s)\le\eta^{-1}B,
\tag{14}
\]
contradicting divergence.  Thus (13)--(14) give precisely
\[
 b\ll u\ll1,\qquad b\ll s\ll1.
\tag{15}
\]
Since
\[
 \frac ub=\frac{d^2}{L^2(n+d)},
\tag{16}
\]
the condition \(u/b\to\infty\) is, up to boundary constants, \(d\gg\sqrt n\,L\) (which also implies \(d\gg L^2\)), while \(u\to0\) is \(d\ll nL\).  This proves the stated dimension description of the wedge.

For the claimed witness, let \(d_n=n\), \(L_n=\log(en)\), and \(\sigma_n=L_n^{-1/2}\).  This sequence is in the stipulated dimension range for all sufficiently large \(n\), and
\[
 b_n=\frac2n,\qquad u_n=\frac1{L_n^2},\qquad s_n=\frac1{L_n}.
\tag{17}
\]
Thus, for all sufficiently large \(n\),
\[
 r_n=\frac1n+\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad
 B_n=\frac2n+\frac1{L_n^3}.
\tag{18}
\]
Because every fixed power of \(L_n\) is \(o(n)\), (18) implies \(r_n/B_n\asymp L_n\to\infty\).

The endpoint \(s=0\) follows from (11), every fixed positive radius from (8), saturation from (9), the small-alphabet regime from (12), and both parametric-dominance elbows from (10)--(11).  The example (17)--(18) proves that the current two theorems do not settle the remaining wedge.  Since the lower benchmark (3) is exactly the product benchmark delivered by the existing radius channel, endpoint arguments and that channel alone cannot decide whether the selector or converse must be sharpened there.
