Set
\[
 L:=\log(en),\qquad a:=\frac1n,\qquad t:=\frac d{n^2},
 \qquad b:=a+t,qquad u:=\frac{d^2}{n^2L^2},
 \qquad s:=\sigma^2.
\tag{1}
\]
First suppose that \(L^2<d<nL\).  Then
\[
 t<u<1,
\tag{2}
\]
because \(u/t=d/L^2>1\) and \(u=(d/(nL))^2<1\).  Hence the upper benchmark in Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\) is
\[
 q=a+\min\{u,s+t\}.
\tag{3}
\]
For arbitrary nonnegative \(s,t,u\) satisfying \(t\le u\),
\[
 \frac12\{t+\min(u,s)\}
 \le \min\{u,s+t\}
 \le t+\min(u,s).
\tag{4}
\]
For the upper inequality, the middle term is at most both \(u\) and \(s+t\), and therefore at most \(t+\min(u,s)\).  For the lower inequality, if \(u\le s+t\), then its left side is at most \((t+u)/2\le u\).  If \(u>s+t\), then \(s<u\), so its left side is \((t+s)/2\le s+t\).  Adding \(a\) to (4), and using \(a\ge0\), gives
\[
 \frac12\{b+\min(u,s)\}
 \le q\le b+\min(u,s).
\tag{5}
\]
Since \(u<1\), the converse benchmark in Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\) is exactly
\[
 \ell=b+s\min(1,u)=b+su.
\tag{6}
\]
This proves the first part of the statement.

We next compare the theorem-level bounds over the entire stipulated dimension range.  Write
\[
 m:=\min(1,u),\qquad
 r:=a+\min\{m,s+t\},\qquad
 B:=b+sm.
\tag{7}
\]
Thus \(r=r_{n,d,\sigma}\), Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\) gives risk at most \(C_{\epsilon}M^2r\), and Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\) gives risk at least \(c_{\epsilon}M^2B\).

If \(\sigma\ge\underline\sigma>0\), then \(s\ge\underline\sigma^2\), whence
\[
 r\le a+m
 \le \max\{1,\underline\sigma^{-2}\}(a+sm)
 \le \max\{1,\underline\sigma^{-2}\}B.
\tag{8}
\]
Combining (8) with the two established risk bounds proves the fixed-positive-radius equivalence, after changing constants only as a function of \(\epsilon\) and \(\underline\sigma\).

The three uniform matching regimes follow from direct comparisons.  If \(u\ge1\), then \(m=1\) and
\[
 r=a+\min(1,s+t)\le a+s+t=b+s=B.
\tag{9}
\]
If \(u\le Cb\) for a fixed \(C\ge0\), then
\[
 r\le a+m\le a+u\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{10}
\]
If \(s\le Cb\) for a fixed \(C\ge0\), then
\[
 r\le a+s+t=b+s\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{11}
\]
For \(C<0\), the latter two conditions are impossible because \(u,b>0\) and \(s\ge0\), so the assertion, when applicable, reduces to (9).  Thus the constants in (9)--(11) are independent of \(\sigma\in[0,2]\), as claimed.

Multiplication by the positive number \(n^2L^2\) gives
\[
 u\le b
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 d^2\le nL^2+dL^2.
\tag{12}
\]
In particular, \(d\le\sqrt n\,L\) implies (12).  If \(d\le L^2\), then \(u=t(d/L^2)\le t\le b\), so the previously noted small-alphabet regime is included.  Equation~(11) applies for every admissible alphabet whenever \(\sigma^2=s\le Cb\).  Equations~(10) and (11) are the two parametric-dominance elbows asserted in the theorem.

It remains to localize possible divergence between the two proved benchmarks.  Suppose along a sequence that \(r/B\) is unbounded.  Equations~(9)--(11) imply that, for every fixed \(C>0\), eventually
\[
 u<1,\qquad u>Cb,\qquad s>Cb.
\tag{13}
\]
Consequently \(b=o(u)\) and \(b=o(s)\).  Equation~(8) rules out every subsequence on which \(s\) is bounded away from zero, so \(s\to0\).  It follows that \(b\to0\), and hence \(t\to0\), because \(0\le t\le b\).  If \(u\) were bounded below by some \(\eta>0\) on a further subsequence, then eventually \(s+t<u\), and (7) would yield
\[
 r=a+s+t=b+s
 \le \max\{1,\eta^{-1}\}(b+\eta s)
 \le \max\{1,\eta^{-1}\}B,
\tag{14}
\]
contradicting divergence.  Hence \(u\to0\).  We have proved that divergence can occur only when
\[
 b\ll u\ll1,
 \qquad b\ll s\ll1.
\tag{15}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \frac ub=\frac{d^2}{L^2(n+d)}.
\tag{16}
\]
Together with \(u=(d/(nL))^2\), relation (16) gives, up to boundary constants in the triangular asymptotic range, \(\sqrt n\,L\ll d\ll nL\), while the second condition in (15) is \(\sigma^2\gg n^{-1}+d/n^2\).

Finally, the wedge is nonempty.  Take \(d_n=n\), \(L_n:=\log(en)\), and \(\sigma_n:=L_n^{-1/2}\).  Then
\[
 b_n=\frac2n,\qquad u_n=\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad s_n=\frac1{L_n}.
\tag{17}
\]
For all sufficiently large \(n\), the numerator and denominator in the theorem's displayed ratio are, respectively,
\[
 r_n=\frac1n+\frac1{L_n^2}\asymp\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad
 B_n=\frac2n+\frac1{L_n^3}\asymp\frac1{L_n^3},
\tag{18}
\]
because \(L_n^3/n\to0\).  Therefore
\[
 \frac{r_n}{B_n}\asymp L_n\longrightarrow\infty.
\tag{19}
\]
The exact-homogeneity endpoint \(s=0\) is covered by (11), every fixed positive radius, including the unrestricted endpoint \(\sigma=2\), is covered by (8), saturation is covered by (9), and the alphabet and elbow claims follow from (10)--(12).  Thus (15) is the only regime not decided by the present upper and lower bounds.  This conclusion compares proved benchmarks only and does not assert that either benchmark is the exact minimax rate inside that wedge.
