Set
\[
 L:=\log(en),\qquad a:=\frac1n,\qquad t:=\frac d{n^2},
 \qquad b:=a+t,\qquad u:=\frac{d^2}{n^2L^2},
 \qquad s:=\sigma^2.
\tag{1}
\]
First suppose that \(L^2<d<nL\).  Then
\[
 t<u<1,
\tag{2}
\]
because \(u/t=d/L^2>1\) and \(u=(d/(nL))^2<1\).  Hence the upper benchmark is
\[
 q=a+\min\{u,s+t\}.
\tag{3}
\]
For nonnegative \(s,t,u\) with \(t\le u\),
\[
 \frac12\{t+\min(u,s)\}
 \le \min\{u,s+t\}
 \le t+\min(u,s).
\tag{4}
\]
For the upper inequality, the middle term is at most both \(u\) and \(s+t\), hence at most \(t+\min(u,s)\).  For the lower inequality, if \(u\le s+t\), its left side is at most \((t+u)/2\le u\); if \(u>s+t\), then \(s<u\), so its left side is \((t+s)/2\le s+t\).  Adding \(a\) to (4) gives
\[
 \frac12\{b+\min(u,s)\}
 \le q\le b+\min(u,s).
\tag{5}
\]
Since \(u<1\), the converse benchmark is
\[
 \ell=b+s\min(1,u)=b+su.
\tag{6}
\]

Over the entire dimension range write
\[
 m:=\min(1,u),\qquad
 r:=a+\min\{m,s+t\},\qquad
 B:=b+sm.
\tag{7}
\]
Thus \(r=r_{n,d,\sigma}\); Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\) bounds the risk above by \(C_\epsilon M^2r\), and Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\) bounds it below by \(c_\epsilon M^2B\).

If \(\sigma\ge\underline\sigma>0\), then \(s\ge\underline\sigma^2\), whence
\[
 r\le a+m
 \le \max\{1,\underline\sigma^{-2}\}(a+sm)
 \le \max\{1,\underline\sigma^{-2}\}B.
\tag{8}
\]
This and the theorem-level bounds prove fixed-positive-radius equivalence.

The three uniform matching regimes follow directly.  If \(u\ge1\), then \(m=1\) and
\[
 r=a+\min(1,s+t)\le a+s+t=b+s=B.
\tag{9}
\]
If \(u\le Cb\), then
\[
 r\le a+m\le a+u\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{10}
\]
If \(s\le Cb\), then
\[
 r\le a+s+t=b+s\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{11}
\]
For \(C<0\), the latter two conditions are impossible, so the assertion, when applicable, reduces to (9).  The constants are independent of \(\sigma\in[0,2]\).

Multiplication by \(n^2L^2>0\) gives
\[
 u\le b
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 d^2\le nL^2+dL^2.
\tag{12}
\]
Thus \(d\le\sqrt n\,L\) implies (12).  If \(d\le L^2\), then \(u=t(d/L^2)\le t\le b\).  Equation (11) applies for every admissible alphabet whenever \(\sigma^2=s\le Cb\).

Suppose along a sequence that \(r/B\) is unbounded.  Equations (9)--(11) imply that, for every fixed finite \(C\), eventually
\[
 u<1,\qquad u>Cb,\qquad s>Cb.
\tag{13}
\]
Consequently \(b=o(u)\) and \(b=o(s)\).  Equation (8) rules out every subsequence on which \(s\) is bounded away from zero, so \(s\to0\).  Hence \(b\to0\) and \(t\to0\).  If \(u\) were bounded below by some \(\eta>0\) on a further subsequence, then eventually \(s+t<u\), and (7) would yield
\[
 r=a+s+t=b+s
 \le \max\{1,\eta^{-1}\}(b+\eta s)
 \le \max\{1,\eta^{-1}\}B,
\tag{14}
\]
contradicting divergence.  Hence \(u\to0\), and divergence can occur only when
\[
 b\ll u\ll1,
 \qquad b\ll s\ll1.
\tag{15}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \frac ub=\frac{d^2}{L^2(n+d)}.
\tag{16}
\]
Together with \(u=(d/(nL))^2\), this gives, up to boundary constants, \(\sqrt n\,L\ll d\ll nL\), while the second condition in (15) is \(\sigma^2\gg n^{-1}+d/n^2\).

Finally take \(d_n=n\), \(L_n:=\log(en)\), and \(\sigma_n:=L_n^{-1/2}\).  Then
\[
 b_n=\frac2n,\qquad u_n=\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad s_n=\frac1{L_n}.
\tag{17}
\]
For all sufficiently large \(n\), the numerator and denominator in the displayed ratio are
\[
 r_n=\frac1n+\frac1{L_n^2}\asymp\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad
 B_n=\frac2n+\frac1{L_n^3}\asymp\frac1{L_n^3},
\tag{18}
\]
because \(L_n^3/n\to0\).  Therefore \(r_n/B_n\asymp L_n\to\infty\).  Exact homogeneity is covered by (11), every fixed positive radius by (8), saturation by (9), and the alphabet and elbow claims by (10)--(12).  Thus (15) is the only regime not decided by the proved bounds; the argument does not assert that either benchmark is the exact minimax rate there.
