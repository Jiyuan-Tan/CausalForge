Put
\[
 a:=\frac1n,\qquad v:=\frac d{n^2},\qquad
 m:=\min(1,u),\qquad s:=\sigma^2,
\tag{1}
\]
so that \(b=a+v\).  The established upper and lower theorems give, with constants depending only on \(\epsilon\),
\[
 c_{\epsilon}M^2 B
 \le \mathsf R_{n,d,\epsilon,M,\sigma}
 \le C_{\epsilon}M^2 r,
\qquad
 B:=b+sm,quad
 r:=a+\min\{m,s+v\}.
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed, the right inequality is Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\), and the left inequality is Theorem~\(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\).

First suppose \(L^2<d<nL\).  Then
\[
 v<u<1,
\tag{3}
\]
because \(u/v=d/L^2>1\) and \(u=(d/(nL))^2<1\).  Hence \(m=u\), \(B=b+su=\ell\), and \(r=q\).  Let \(w:=\min(u,s)\).  The elementary inequality
\[
 \min\{u,s+v\}\le v+\min(u,s)=v+w
\tag{4}
\]
gives \(q\le b+w\).  Conversely, (3) implies
\[
 \min\{u,s+v\}\ge v
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 \min\{u,s+v\}\ge w.
\tag{5}
\]
Thus \(q\ge b\) and \(q\ge a+w\ge w\).  Therefore
\[
 q\ge\max(b,w)\ge\frac12(b+w).
\tag{6}
\]
Equations (4)--(6) prove the two triangular-regime inequalities and the displayed identity for \(\ell\).

Now fix \(\underline\sigma\in(0,2]\) and suppose \(\sigma\ge\underline\sigma\).  From (1)--(2),
\[
 B=b+sm\ge a+\underline\sigma^2m
 \ge \min(1,\underline\sigma^2)(a+m),
 \qquad r\le a+m.
\tag{7}
\]
The lower half of (2) and (7) give the asserted lower multiple of \(M^2r_{n,d,\sigma}\), while the upper half of (2) gives the asserted upper multiple.  This proves matching at every fixed positive interior radius, uniformly over the full stipulated dimension range.

We next prove the three radius-uniform matching regimes.  If \(u\ge1\), then \(m=1\) and
\[
 r=a+\min\{1,s+v\}\le a+s+v=b+s=B.
\tag{8}
\]
If \(u\le Cb\), then \(m\le u\le Cb\), whence
\[
 r\le a+m\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{9}
\]
If \(s\le Cb\), then
\[
 r\le a+s+v=b+s\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)B.
\tag{10}
\]
Combining each of (8)--(10) with (2) proves the claimed equivalence with constants independent of \(\sigma\in[0,2]\).

The condition \(u\le b\) is equivalent, after multiplying by the positive number \(n^2L^2\), to
\[
 d^2\le nL^2+dL^2.
\tag{11}
\]
If \(d\le\sqrt n\,L\), then \(d^2\le nL^2\), so (11) holds.  If \(d\le L^2\), then \(u/v=d/L^2\le1\), so \(u\le v\le b\).  Finally, (10) applies to every admissible alphabet whenever \(\sigma^2=s\le Cb\).  This proves all stated sufficient conditions.

For completeness, consider any triangular sequence along which the numerical ratio \(r/B\) in (2) diverges.  The preceding bounds already show that neither \(u\ge1\), nor \(u=O(b)\), nor \(s=O(b)\) can occur along a subsequence.  In fact, \(u\to0\) and \(s\to0\) are also necessary.  To see the first assertion, if \(m\ge\eta>0\) along a subsequence, then
\[
 r\le b+s,qquad B=b+sm\ge b+\eta s,
\tag{12}
\]
so \(r/B\le\eta^{-1}\).  Thus \(m\to0\), hence \(u\to0\).  If \(s\ge\eta>0\) along a subsequence, then
\[
 r\le b+m,qquad B=b+sm\ge b+\eta m,
\tag{13}
\]
again bounding the ratio by \(\eta^{-1}\).  Hence \(s\to0\).  Together with the exclusions \(u=O(b)\) and \(s=O(b)\), divergence is possible only when
\[
 b\ll u\ll1,
 \qquad b\ll s\ll1.
\tag{14}
\]
Moreover,
\[
 \frac ub=\frac{d^2}{L^2(n+d)},
\tag{15}
\]
so (14), up to constant-width boundaries, is precisely \(\sqrt n\,L\ll d\ll nL\), together with \(s\gg b\).

The wedge is nonempty.  Take \(d_n=n\), \(L_n=\log(en)\), and \(\sigma_n=L_n^{-1/2}\).  Then
\[
 b_n=\frac2n,qquad u_n=\frac1{L_n^2},qquad s_n=\frac1{L_n}.
\tag{16}
\]
Since powers of \(L_n\) are \(o(n)\), the numerator and denominator in the theorem reduce asymptotically to
\[
 r_n=\frac1n+\frac1{L_n^2}\asymp\frac1{L_n^2},
 \qquad
 B_n=\frac2n+\frac1{L_n^3}\asymp\frac1{L_n^3}.
\tag{17}
\]
Therefore \(r_n/B_n\asymp L_n\to\infty\).  Equations (7)--(17) establish every claimed matched region and show that only the stated shrinking-radius wedge remains undecided by the current selector and channel converse.  In particular, \(s=0\) is covered by (10), \(s=4\) by (7), saturation by (8), the small-alphabet regime by (9), and the two parametric-dominance elbows by (9)--(10).
