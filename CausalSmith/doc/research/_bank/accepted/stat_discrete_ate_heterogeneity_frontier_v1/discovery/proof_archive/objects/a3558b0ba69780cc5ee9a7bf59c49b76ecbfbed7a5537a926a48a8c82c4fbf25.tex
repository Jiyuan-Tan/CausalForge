Let
\[
 L:=\log(en),\qquad x:=\frac d{n^2},\qquad
 b:=\frac1n+x,\qquad u:=\frac{d^2}{n^2L^2},
 \qquad s:=\sigma^2,
\]
and put \(v:=\min(1,u)\).  Define
\[
 q:=\frac1n+\min\{v,s+x\},
 \qquad
 \ell:=b+sv.
\tag{1}
\]
By the definition of \(r_{n,d,\sigma}\), one has \(q=r_{n,d,\sigma}\).  Theorems~\(\mathrm{thm:frontier\text{-}upper}\) and \(\mathrm{thm:radius\text{-}channel\text{-}converse}\), respectively, give constants depending only on \(\epsilon\) such that
\[
 \mathsf R_{n,d,\epsilon,M,\sigma}\le C_{\epsilon}M^2q,
 \qquad
 \mathsf R_{n,d,\epsilon,M,\sigma}\ge c_{\epsilon}M^2\ell.
\tag{2}
\]
Their common support, overlap, fourth-moment, radius, and dimension hypotheses are exactly the hypotheses of the theorem.  It therefore remains only to compare the deterministic quantities in (1).

First consider the nonsaturated triangular regime \(L^2<d<nL\).  Then
\[
 0<x<u<1,
\tag{3}
\]
because \(u/x=d/L^2>1\) and \(u<1\).  Let \(m:=\min(u,s)\).  We claim
\[
 \frac{x+m}{2}\le\min\{u,s+x\}\le x+m.
\tag{4}
\]
For the upper inequality, if \(m=s\), then the second argument of the minimum is \(x+m\); if \(m=u\), then the first argument is \(m\le x+m\).  For the lower inequality, (3) and \(m\le u\) imply \(x+m<2u\), while \(s+x\ge m+x\).  Thus both arguments of the minimum are at least \((x+m)/2\), proving (4).  Adding \(n^{-1}\) and using \(b=n^{-1}+x\) gives
\[
 \frac12\{b+min(u,s)\}\le q\le b+min(u,s).
\tag{5}
\]
Since \(v=u\) in this regime, (1) also gives the exact identity
\[
 \ell=b+su.
\tag{6}
\]
This proves the asserted benchmark reduction.

Now fix \(\underline\sigma\in(0,2]\) and suppose \(\sigma\ge\underline\sigma\).  From (1),
\[
 q\le\frac1n+v,
 \qquad
 \ell\ge\frac1n+\underline\sigma^2v.
\]
Hence
\[
 q\le\max\{1,\underline\sigma^{-2}\}\ell.
\tag{7}
\]
Combining (7) with (2) proves the two-sided rate equivalence for every fixed interior lower radius.

If \(u\ge1\), then \(v=1\).  When \(s+x\le1\), equation (1) gives \(q=\ell\).  When \(s+x>1\), it gives \(q=n^{-1}+1\le\ell\).  Thus in either case \(q\le\ell\), and (2) proves matching throughout the saturation regime, uniformly in \(\sigma\).

Let \(C<\infty\) be fixed.  If \(u\le Cb\), then \(v\le u\) and
\[
 q\le\frac1n+v\le b+u\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)\ell.
\tag{8}
\]
If instead \(s\le Cb\), then
\[
 q\le\frac1n+s+x=b+s\le(1+C)b\le(1+C)\ell.
\tag{9}
\]
Equations (2), (8), and (9) establish uniform matching at both parametric-dominance elbows.  Moreover,
\[
 u\le b
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 \frac{d^2}{n^2L^2}\le\frac1n+\frac d{n^2}
 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
 d^2\le nL^2+dL^2.
\tag{10}
\]
The condition \(d\le\sqrt n\,L\) makes the first term on the right of the last inequality dominate \(d^2\), and \(d\le L^2\) gives \(d^2\le dL^2\).  Both are therefore sufficient.  Equation (9) does not restrict \(d\), beyond the theorem's standing admissible range, so it proves matching for every alphabet whenever \(\sigma^2\le Cb\).

For completeness, we now localize every sequence on which the numerical ratio \(q/\ell\) can diverge.  Equations (8)--(9) show that such a sequence must satisfy
\[
 \frac ub\longrightarrow\infty,
 \qquad
 \frac sb\longrightarrow\infty
\tag{11}
\]
along a subsequence.  It must also have \(u\to0\) and \(s\to0\).  Indeed, if \(u\ge c>0\), then, with \(m=\min(u,s)\), one has \(su\ge c' m\) for \(c':=\min(c,1)\): when \(s\le u\), \(su\ge cs\), while when \(s>u\), \(su>u^2\ge cu\).  Equations (5)--(6) then bound \(q/\ell\) by a constant depending only on \(c\).  The same conclusion follows from (7) if \(s\ge c>0\).  Saturation was already handled above.  Therefore divergence is possible only when
\[
 b\ll u\ll1,
 \qquad b\ll s\ll1.
\tag{12}
\]
The first relation in (12) is
\[
 d^2\gg nL^2+dL^2,
 \qquad d^2\ll n^2L^2.
\]
Since \(\sqrt n/L\to\infty\), this is equivalent up to boundary constants to \(\sqrt n\,L\ll d\ll nL\).  The second relation is precisely \(\sigma^2\gg n^{-1}+d/n^2\) together with \(\sigma\to0\).

Finally, take \(d_n=n\), \(L_n=log(en)\), and \(\sigma_n=L_n^{-1/2}\).  For all sufficiently large \(n\), the support, radius, and dimension boundaries hold, and
\[
 b_n=\frac2n,qquad u_n=L_n^{-2},qquad s_n=L_n^{-1}.
\]
Thus
\[
 q_n=\frac1n+L_n^{-2}\asymp L_n^{-2},
 \qquad
 \ell_n=\frac2n+L_n^{-3}\asymp L_n^{-3},
\]
so \(q_n/\ell_n\asymp L_n\to\infty\).  This witness lies inside (12).  The algebra proves the sharper localization but does not choose between a stronger lower bound and a sharper estimator inside that wedge.  No inference, coverage, or adaptation to unknown \(M\) or \(\sigma\) is asserted.
