\textbf{Proof.}
Fix a member law \(P\) and write \(R(u)=(1,u,\ldots,u^p)^\top\). For every nonzero \(c\in\mathbb R^{p+1}\), the polynomial \(c^\top R(x/h)\) is nonzero except at finitely many points. Since \(f_S(x)=x^ag_S(x)>0\) for Lebesgue-almost every \(x\in(0,h)\),
\[
c^\top G_h^{\mathrm o}c=\int_0^h\{c^\top R(x/h)\}^2f_S(x)\,dx>0.
\]
Thus \(G_h^{\mathrm o}\) is positive definite and the inverse in the definition exists.

If \(q(x)=d^\top R(x/h)\) has degree at most \(p\), symmetry of \(G_h^{\mathrm o}\) and its defining normal equation give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^h w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}}(x;P)q(x)f_S(x)\,dx
&=d^\top G_h^{\mathrm o}\{G_h^{\mathrm o}\}^{-1}q_h^{\mathrm o}\\
&=d^\top q_h^{\mathrm o}
=\int_0^h q(x)f_T(x)\,dx.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]
This proves exact polynomial reproduction.

Rescale \(x=hu\). Then
\[
G_h^{\mathrm o}=h^{a+1}A_h,
\qquad
A_h=\int_0^1R(u)R(u)^\top u^ag_S(hu)\,du,
\qquad
q_h^{\mathrm o}=h v_h,
\quad
v_h=\int_0^1R(u)g_T(hu)\,du.
\tag{2}
\]
The boundary-factor bounds imply, in Loewner order,
\[
c_{S,-}A_a\preceq A_h\preceq c_{S,+}A_a,
\qquad
A_a=\int_0^1R(u)R(u)^\top u^a\,du.
\tag{3}
\]
The same polynomial argument used above shows that \(A_a\) is positive definite. Hence the eigenvalues of every \(A_h\) are bounded above and away from zero by constants depending only on the fixed class constants. Moreover \(\|v_h\|_2\) is uniformly bounded above, while its first coordinate is at least \(c_{T,-}\). It follows from (2)--(3) that
\[
\{q_h^{\mathrm o}\}^\top\{G_h^{\mathrm o}\}^{-1}q_h^{\mathrm o}
=h^{1-a}v_h^\top A_h^{-1}v_h\asymp h^{1-a}.
\tag{4}
\]
Because the left side of (4) equals \(\int_0^h\{w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}}(x;P)\}^2f_S(x)\,dx\), Cauchy--Schwarz and \(\int_0^hf_S(x)\,dx\le c_{S,+}h^{a+1}/(a+1)\) yield
\[
\int_0^h|w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}}(x;P)|f_S(x)\,dx
\le
\left(\int_0^h(w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}})^2f_S\right)^{1/2}
\left(\int_0^hf_S\right)^{1/2}
\le Ch.
\tag{5}
\]

Let \(v\in\mathcal H^s(2M)\) with \(\|v\|_\infty\le1\), and let \(T_pv\) be its order-\(p\) Taylor polynomial at zero. The stated H\"older convention and the integral Taylor remainder give
\[
\sup_{0\le x\le h}|v(x)-T_pv(x)|\le C_{s,M}h^s.
\tag{6}
\]
By reproduction (1), the signed measure in \(b_h(v;P)\) annihilates \(T_pv\). Combining (5), (6), and \(\int_0^hf_T\le c_{T,+}h\) proves
\[
|b_h(v;P)|
\le C_{s,M}h^s\left\{\int_0^h|w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}}|f_S+\int_0^hf_T\right\}
\le Ch^{s+1}.
\tag{7}
\]
Taking the supremum proves the uniform actual-bias upper bound.

For sharpness when \(a>0\), take the admissible constant factors \(g_S\equiv a+1\) and \(g_T\equiv1\), subject to the fixed bounds. In rescaled coordinates the signed boundary measure has density
\[
d\nu_a(u)=\{(a+1)u^a\widetilde w_a(u)-1\}\,du,
\qquad 0<u<1,
\tag{8}
\]
where \(\widetilde w_a\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(p\). This signed measure is not zero: otherwise \(\widetilde w_a(u)=\{(a+1)u^a\}^{-1}\) almost everywhere, which is impossible for a polynomial when \(a>0\). Hence there is \(\phi\in C_c^\infty(0,1)\) with \(\int\phi\,d\nu_a\ne0\). After multiplying \(\phi\) by a sufficiently small fixed constant, the functions
\[
v_h(x)=h^s\phi(x/h)\mathbf 1\{0\le x\le h\}
\tag{9}
\]
belong to \(\mathcal H^s(2M)\) and have sup norm at most one. Equations (8)--(9) give
\[
|b_h(v_h;P)|=h^{s+1}\left|\int_0^1\phi(u)\,d\nu_a(u)\right|\ge ch^{s+1}.
\tag{10}
\]
The density law can be completed with randomized treatment and bounded Bernoulli potential outcomes of mean one half, whose variance is one quarter and therefore at least \(M^{-1}\) because \(M>4\). It is thus a member of the model class. This proves the stated class sharpness for \(a>0\). When \(a=0\) and \(f_S=f_T\), the normal equations give \(w_h^{\mathrm{BLP}}\equiv1\) on \([0,h)\), so the bias is exactly zero; this verifies the advertised boundary qualification.

Finally, the two summands defining \(\omega_h^{\mathrm o}\) have disjoint supports. The boundary squared norm is the quadratic form in (4), and the interior squared norm is
\[
\int_h^1r(x)^2f_S(x)\,dx=\int_h^1\frac{f_T(x)^2}{f_S(x)}\,dx.
\]
Adding them proves the exact norm identity. \(\square\)
