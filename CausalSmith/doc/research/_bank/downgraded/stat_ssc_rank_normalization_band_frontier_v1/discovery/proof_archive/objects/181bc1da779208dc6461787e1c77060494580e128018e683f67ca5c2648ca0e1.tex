\textbf{Proof.}
By \(\mathrm{lem:normalization\mbox{-}collapse}\), all donor-post event
survival curves equal \(s_1\), hence all donor-post failure hazards equal a
common function \(\lambda_1\).  The censoring survivals \(G_{1,m}\) need
not be equal.  For \(i=(m,j)\), define
\[
 N_i(u)=\mathbf1\{X_{1,m,j}\le u,\Delta_{1,m,j}=1\},
 \quad Y_i(u)=\mathbf1\{X_{1,m,j}\ge u\},
 \quad M_i(u)=N_i(u)-\int_0^uY_i(v)\lambda_1(v)\,dv.       \tag{1}
\]
Control consistency and independent censoring identify the compensator in
(1); patient independence makes the \(M_i\)'s independent mean-zero
square-integrable martingales.  Put
\[
 Y=\sum_iY_i,
 \quad M=\sum_iM_i,
 \quad J_Y=\mathbf1\{Y>0\},
 \quad \bar G_D=N_0^{-1}\sum_mG_{1,m},
 \quad H_D=s_1\bar G_D.                                    \tag{2}
\]
Because each donor contributes exactly \(K_D\) patients,
\[
 EY(u)=K_D\sum_m s_1(u)G_{1,m}(u)=n_DH_D(u),
 \qquad H_D(u)\ge h_0:=s_-g_->0.                           \tag{3}
\]

Let \(\widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}\) be the product-limit curve formed after
pooling the records, and set \(R=\widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}/s_1\).  The
product-integral equations and integration by parts give the exact ratio
identity
\[
 R(t)-1=-\int_0^tR(u-)\frac{J_Y(u)}{Y(u)}\,dM(u)
 +\int_0^tR(u-)\{1-J_Y(u)\}\lambda_1(u)\,du.               \tag{4}
\]
The convention \(J_Y/Y=0\) on an empty risk set makes (4) well defined.
Event overlap and the range of a product-limit curve give
\[
 0\le R\le s_-^{-1}.                                       \tag{5}
\]
Define
\[
 \phi_i^{\mathrm{pool}}(t)
 =-s_1(t)\int_0^tH_D(u)^{-1}\,dM_i(u).                     \tag{6}
\]
The integrand is deterministic and bounded by \(h_0^{-1}\), so these
processes are independent and mean zero.  Subtracting
\(n_D^{-1}\sum_i\phi_i^{\mathrm{pool}}\) from (4) yields
\[
 \widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}-s_1-n_D^{-1}\sum_i\phi_i^{\mathrm{pool}}
 =V_n+A_n,                                                   \tag{7}
\]
where, writing \(n=n_D\),
\[
 \begin{split}
 V_n(t)&=-s_1(t)\int_0^tD_n(u)\,dM(u),\\
 D_n(u)&=R(u-)\frac{J_Y(u)}{Y(u)}-\frac1{nH_D(u)},\\
 A_n(t)&=s_1(t)\int_0^tR(u-)\{1-J_Y(u)\}\lambda_1(u)\,du.
 \end{split}                                                \tag{8}
\]

The risk indicators are independent but non-identically distributed.
Their aggregate at the horizon is Poisson--binomial and, by (3),
\[
 P\{Y(\bar\tau)=0\}
 =\prod_i\{1-EY_i(\bar\tau)\}
 \le\exp\{-EY(\bar\tau)\}
 \le e^{-h_0n}.                                             \tag{9}
\]
Since \(A_n\) vanishes off this event and is uniformly bounded by
\(s_-^{-1}L\bar\tau\),
\[
 \|EA_n\|_\infty\le Ce^{-h_0n},
 \qquad E\|A_n-EA_n\|_\infty^2\le Ce^{-h_0n}.              \tag{10}
\]

For completeness, the same exponential-Markov calculation for a sum of
independent Bernoulli variables gives the Poisson--binomial Chernoff bound
\[
 P(\mathcal E_n^c)\le e^{-c_1n},
 \qquad
 \mathcal E_n=\{Y(\bar\tau)\ge nh_0/2\}.                  \tag{11}
\]
Indeed, for \(s>0\), independence and \(1+x\le e^x\) imply
\[
 E e^{-sY}\le
 \exp\{(e^{-s}-1)EY\};
\]
exponential Markov inequality at \(nh_0/2\), followed by a fixed small
choice of \(s\), proves (11).  On \(\mathcal E_n\), monotonicity of the
risk set gives \(Y(u)\ge nh_0/2\) for all \(u\le\bar\tau\), and
\[
 D_n(u)=\frac{R(u-)-1}{Y(u)}
 -\frac{Y(u)-nH_D(u)}{nH_D(u)Y(u)}.                         \tag{12}
\]
Stop (4) when \(Y\) first falls below \(nh_0/2\).  Before that time the
exhaustion term is zero and the predictable quadratic variation of its
martingale part is bounded by \(C/n\).  Doob's square-integrable maximal
inequality, whose elementary proof follows from applying optional stopping
to the first crossing of a level and integrating the resulting tail bound,
therefore gives
\[
 E\{\mathbf1_{\mathcal E_n}\|R-1\|_\infty^2\}\le Cn^{-1}.
                                                                    \tag{13}
\]
For each \(u\), independence and boundedness of the non-identical risk
indicators give
\[
 E\{Y(u)-nH_D(u)\}^2
 =\sum_i\operatorname{Var}\{Y_i(u)\}\le n.                \tag{14}
\]
Substitution of (12)--(14) into the martingale isometry yields
\[
 E\left[\mathbf1_{\mathcal E_n}
   \int_0^{\bar\tau}D_n(u)^2Y(u)\lambda_1(u)\,du\right]
 \le Cn^{-2}.                                               \tag{15}
\]
On \(\mathcal E_n^c\), (3), (5), and \(J_Y/Y\le1\) whenever
\(J_Y=1\) give a fixed bound for the integrand; (11) then gives the same
right side after increasing \(C\).  A second application of the maximal
inequality proves
\[
 E\|V_n\|_\infty^2\le Cn^{-2}.                             \tag{16}
\]
Set \(b_{D,n}^{\mathrm{PL}}=EA_n\) and
\(q_{D,n}^{\mathrm{PL}}=V_n+A_n-EA_n\).  Equations
(7), (10), and (16) prove the asserted step-curve expansion.  Applying the
deterministic contraction \(\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}\) to every term
preserves the bias and second-moment bounds and gives the claimed continuous
expansion.  At each grid knot \(\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}s_1=s_1=\theta\),
so there is no interpolation term in the gridwise identity.

We next validate the multiplier.  Its un-interpolated form is
\[
 \widehat Z_1^\xi(t)
 =-\widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}(t-)
   \sum_i\xi_i\int_0^t\frac{J_Y(u)}{Y(u)}\,dN_i(u).         \tag{17}
\]
Conditional on the records, different observed failure jumps in (17) carry
independent Gaussian multipliers.  Thus the partial sums ordered by failure
time form a conditional Gaussian martingale.  On \(\mathcal E_n\), (5)
and \(Y\ge nh_0/2\) give
\[
 E_\xi\langle\widehat Z_1^\xi\rangle(\bar\tau)
 \le \frac{C}{n^2}\sum_iN_i(\bar\tau).
\]
The conditional maximal inequality and \(\sum_iN_i\le n\) imply
\(E_\xi\|\widehat Z_1^\xi\|_\infty^2\le C/n\) on
\(\mathcal E_n\).  On its complement, the product-limit derivative is
bounded by the convention in the definition and (11) controls the
expectation.  Interpolation is a supremum-norm contraction, hence
\[
 \sup_PE_PE_\xi\|\widehat Z_1^\xi\|_{\infty,\mathcal G}^2
 \le Cn^{-1}.                                               \tag{18}
\]

Let \(Z_1=n^{-1}\sum_i\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}phi_i^{\mathrm{pool}}\),
and let \(\Sigma(g,g')=E\{Z_1(g)Z_1(g')\}\).  Conditional covariance in
(17) is the optional-variation plug-in
\[
 \widehat\Sigma(g,g')=
 \widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}(g)\widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}(g')
 \sum_i\int_0^{g\wedge g'}\frac{J_Y(u)}{Y(u)^2}\,dN_i(u), \tag{19}
\]
followed by interpolation in both arguments.  Subtract the deterministic
counterpart obtained by replacing \(Y/n\) with \(H_D\),
\(\widehat S_D^{\mathrm{PL}}\) with \(s_1\), and \(dN_i\) with its
compensator.  On \(\mathcal E_n\), the identity
\[
 Y^{-2}-(nH_D)^{-2}
 =\{nH_D-Y\}\{nH_D+Y\}/\{Y^2(nH_D)^2\}                   \tag{20}
\]
together with (13)--(14), martingale isometry, and a union bound over the
at most \(J^2\) grid pairs gives
\[
 E\max_{g,g'}|\widehat\Sigma(g,g')-\Sigma(g,g')|
 \le Cn^{-3/2}\log(e+J)+Ce^{-c_1n}.                       \tag{21}
\]
Here the logarithm follows by exponential Markov inequality applied to the
bounded martingale increments; no identical-censoring step is used.

We finally prove the additive random-quantile assertion.  Replace the
absolute maximum over \(J\) coordinates by the maximum over the signed
\(2J\)-vector.  For \(a>0\), define
\[
 F_a(x)=a^{-1}\log\sum_{k=1}^{2J}e^{ax_k}.
\]
Then \(\max_kx_k\le F_a(x)\le\max_kx_k+a^{-1}\log(2J)\),
and direct differentiation of the softmax weights gives
\[
 \|D^2F_a\|_1\le Ca,
 \qquad \|D^3F_a\|_1\le Ca^2.                             \tag{22}
\]
Compose \(F_a\) with a one-Lipschitz ramp which changes from one to zero
over an interval of length \(x/2\), mollified by a triangular kernel.
Its first three derivative bounds are \(C x^{-k}\).  Choose
\(a=4\log(2J)/x\).  Taylor expansion to third order while replacing the
independent, centered vectors \(n^{-1}\phi_i\) one at a time by Gaussian
vectors with the same covariance yields
\[
 \sup_y|E\mathcal R_{y,x}(Z_1)-E\mathcal R_{y,x}(G_\Sigma)|
 \le Cx^{-1}n^{-2/3}\log^2(e+n),                           \tag{23}
\]
after truncating each bounded martingale vector at
\(n^{1/6}/\log(e+n)\) in standard-error units.  To see the exponent
directly, the sum of unnormalized third moments is \(O(n)\), the weights
are \(n^{-1}\), and the third-order Taylor sum is \(O(n^{-2})\); taking a
ramp width no smaller than \(n^{-2/3}\log^2(e+n)\) makes the right side of
(22) summable.  The discarded part has the same bound by exponential
Markov inequality for the bounded martingale increments.  The corrected
grid gives \(J\le Cn^{1/(2q)}\), so every \(\log J\) factor is absorbed by
the displayed \(\log^2(e+n)\).

Gaussian interpolation between \(G_\Sigma\) and the conditional Gaussian
vector in (17), followed by (21), gives the same bound: differentiating
the Gaussian interpolation identity uses only the second derivative in
(22), and the covariance error is multiplied by
\(C x^{-2}\log(2J)\).  A ghost copy of the records makes this calculation
conditional; replacing the ghost covariance and derivative by their
observed counterparts uses (13), (20), and (21).  Integrating out the
ghost sample gives, uniformly in the ramp center \(y\),
\[
 E\mathcal R_{y,x}(Z_1)
 \le E E_\xi\mathcal R_{y,x/2}(\widehat Z_1^\xi)
   +C\chi_1/x,
 \qquad \chi_1=n^{-2/3}\log^2(e+n).                        \tag{24}
\]
This is the finite-grid multiplier comparison; its proof is a direct
Taylor replacement, not an appeal to anti-concentration.

Let \(c_{1,q_0}^\xi\) be the left-continuous conditional \(q_0\)-quantile.
Apply (24) first to a simple-function upper approximation of this random
quantile, use the defining inequality
\(P_\xi\{\|\widehat Z_1^\xi\|\le c_{1,q_0}^\xi\}\ge q_0\),
and pass monotonically to the quantile.  The ramp equals one on the event
\(\{\|Z_1\|>c_{1,q_0}^\xi+x\}\) and its multiplier complement has
conditional expectation at most \(1-q_0\).  Therefore
\[
 P\{\|Z_1\|_{infty,\mathcal G}>c_{1,q_0}^\xi+x\}
 \le1-q_0+C\chi_1/x.                                      \tag{25}
\]
The use of a ramp rather than a distributional inverse is what permits
zero-variance coordinates, in particular the normalization knot at zero.
Equations (18), (21), and (25) complete the proof. \(\square\)
