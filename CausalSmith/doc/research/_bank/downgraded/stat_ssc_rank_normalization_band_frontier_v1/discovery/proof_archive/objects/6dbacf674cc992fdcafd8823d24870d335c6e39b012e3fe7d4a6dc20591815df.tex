\textbf{Proof of the proposed corrected statement.}
Fix one observed control cell and suppress its \((p,m)\) subscripts.  Write
\[
 N_j(u)=\mathbf 1\{X_j\le u,\Delta_j=1\},\qquad
 Y_j(u)=\mathbf 1\{X_j\ge u\},\qquad
 Y(u)=\sum_{j=1}^kY_j(u),
\]
using the predictable left-continuous version of \(Y_j\) at a jump time.  By \(\mathrm{ass:independent-censoring}\), the compensator of \(N_j\) is
\[
 \int_0^uY_j(v)\lambda^0(v)\,dv.
\]
Consequently,
\[
 M_j(u)=N_j(u)-\int_0^uY_j(v)\lambda^0(v)\,dv
\]
is a mean-zero square-integrable martingale.  By \(\mathrm{ass:patient-independence}\), these martingales are independent.  Put
\[
 M=\sum_{j=1}^kM_j,\qquad
 J(u)=\mathbf 1\{Y(u)>0\},\qquad
 H(u)=E Y_j(u)=S^0(u)G(u).
\]
The last equality follows from independent censoring; the continuous event hazard makes \(\Pr(T^0\ge u)=\Pr(T^0>u)\).  By \(\mathrm{ass:censor-overlap}\) and \(\mathrm{ass:event-overlap}\),
\[
 H(u)\ge h_0:=s_-g_->0,qquad u\in\mathcal I.                 \tag{1}
\]
Moreover, \(\mathrm{ass:hazard-smoothness}\) gives
\[
 \sup_{u\in\mathcal I}\lambda^0(u)\le L,qquad
 \Lambda^0(\bar\tau):=\int_0^{\bar\tau}\lambda^0(u)\,du
 \le L\bar\tau.                                               \tag{2}
\]

Let \(\widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}\) be the product-limit step curve and set \(R=\widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}/S^0\).  The product-integral equations are
\[
 d\widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}(u)
 =-\widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}(u-)\frac{J(u)}{Y(u)}\,dN(u),
 \qquad
 dS^0(u)=-S^0(u)\lambda^0(u)\,du,
\]
where \(J/Y\) is defined to be zero on an empty risk set.  Integration by parts and the identity \(dN=dM+Y\lambda^0du\) yield the exact relation
\[
 R(t)-1
 =-\int_0^tR(u-)\frac{J(u)}{Y(u)}\,dM(u)
  +\int_0^tR(u-)\{1-J(u)\}\lambda^0(u)\,du.                 \tag{3}
\]
No division by zero occurs in (3).  Since both \(0\le\widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}\le1\) and \(S^0\ge s_-\),
\[
 0\le R(u)\le s_-^{-1}.                                      \tag{4}
\]

Define the un-interpolated patient influence process by
\[
 \varphi_j^{\mathrm{PL}}(t)
 =-S^0(t)\int_0^tH(u)^{-1}\,dM_j(u).                         \tag{5}
\]
The integrand is deterministic and bounded by \(h_0^{-1}\), so each process in (5) is mean zero, and the processes are independent across patients.  Subtracting \(k^{-1}\sum_j\varphi_j^{\mathrm{PL}}\) from (3), after multiplication by \(S^0(t)\), gives
\[
 \widehat S^{\mathrm{PL}}(t)-S^0(t)
 -k^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^k\varphi_j^{\mathrm{PL}}(t)
 =V_k(t)+A_k(t),                                               \tag{6}
\]
where
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 V_k(t)&=-S^0(t)\int_0^tD_k(u)\,dM(u),\\
 D_k(u)&=R(u-)\frac{J(u)}{Y(u)}-\frac{1}{kH(u)},\\
 A_k(t)&=S^0(t)\int_0^tR(u-)\{1-J(u)\}\lambda^0(u)\,du.
 \end{aligned}                                                 \tag{7}
\]
All integrands in (5) and (7) are predictable.

We first isolate the bias.  The stochastic integral in \(V_k\) is integrable and has expectation zero.  Since the risk indicators are nonincreasing in time, \(A_k\) can be nonzero only on
\[
 \mathcal X_k:=\{Y(\bar\tau)=0\}.
\]
Independence across patients and (1) imply
\[
 \Pr(\mathcal X_k)=\{1-H(\bar\tau)\}^k
 \le e^{-kH(\bar\tau)}\le e^{-h_0k}.                         \tag{8}
\]
Using (2), (4), and Fubini's theorem,
\[
 \|EA_k\|_\infty
 \le s_-^{-1}L\bar\tau e^{-h_0k}.                            \tag{9}
\]
Set
\[
 b_k^{\mathrm{PL}}=EA_k,qquad
 q_k^{\mathrm{PL}}=V_k+A_k-EA_k.                             \tag{10}
\]
Equations (6) and (10) give the required algebraic decomposition for the step curve, and \(Eq_k^{\mathrm{PL}}=0\).  Since \(c_0=h_0/3<h_0\), a fixed enlargement of a constant gives
\[
 \|b_k^{\mathrm{PL}}\|_\infty\le C_0e^{-c_0k}.             \tag{11}
\]

We next prove the second-order moment.  Let
\[
 \mathcal E_k=\{Y(\bar\tau)\ge kh_0/2\}.
\]
Because \(Y(\bar\tau)\) is binomial with success probability \(H(\bar\tau)\ge h_0\), the exponential Markov bound applied to \(\exp\{-sY(\bar\tau)\}\), optimized over \(s>0\), gives
\[
 \Pr(\mathcal E_k^c)\le e^{-c_1k}                            \tag{12}
\]
for a fixed \(c_1=c_1(h_0)>0\).  On \(\mathcal E_k\), monotonicity gives \(Y(u)\ge kh_0/2\) and \(J(u)=1\) for every \(u\in\mathcal I\), and hence
\[
 D_k(u)=\frac{R(u-)-1}{Y(u)}
 -\frac{Y(u)-kH(u)}{kH(u)Y(u)}.                              \tag{13}
\]

Stop (3) when the risk set first falls below \(kh_0/2\).  Before that time its exhaustion term is zero.  The predictable quadratic variation of the stopped stochastic integral is at most
\[
 \int_0^{\bar\tau}R(u-)^2Y(u)^{-1}\lambda^0(u)\,du
 \le Ck^{-1}.                                                 \tag{14}
\]
The elementary square-integrable martingale maximal inequality follows by applying optional stopping at the first crossing of a level and then Cauchy--Schwarz; it gives \(E\sup_{u\le\bar\tau}|U(u)|^2\le4E\langle U\rangle(\bar\tau)\).  Applying it to (14) yields
\[
 E\bigl[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal E_k}\|R-1\|_\infty^2\bigr]
 \le Ck^{-1}.                                                 \tag{15}
\]
For every fixed \(u\), patient independence gives
\[
 E\{Y(u)-kH(u)\}^2=kH(u)\{1-H(u)\}\le k.                  \tag{16}
\]
Using (13), \(Y\ge kh_0/2\), (2), (15), and then (16), we obtain
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 E\left[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal E_k}
   \int_0^{\bar\tau}D_k(u)^2Y(u)\lambda^0(u)\,du\right]
 &\le \frac{C}{k}E\bigl[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal E_k}\|R-1\|_\infty^2\bigr]\\
 &\quad+\frac{C}{k^3}\int_0^{\bar\tau}
       E\{Y(u)-kH(u)\}^2\lambda^0(u)\,du\\
 &\le Ck^{-2}.                                                
 \end{aligned}                                                 \tag{17}
\]
On \(\mathcal E_k^c\), (1), (2), and (4) give
\[
 D_k(u)^2Y(u)
 \le 2s_-^{-2}\frac{J(u)}{Y(u)}+\frac{2Y(u)}{k^2h_0^2}
 \le C,
\]
so (12) implies
\[
 E\left[\mathbf 1_{\mathcal E_k^c}
   \int_0^{\bar\tau}D_k(u)^2Y(u)\lambda^0(u)\,du\right]
 \le Ce^{-c_1k}\le Ck^{-2}.                                 \tag{18}
\]
Equations (17)--(18) and the martingale maximal inequality show
\[
 E\|V_k\|_\infty^2\le Ck^{-2}.                              \tag{19}
\]
For the complement event in this maximal calculation one may alternatively use (6): every survival curve and \(A_k\) is uniformly bounded, while the average influence process in (5) is deterministically bounded by \(h_0^{-1}(1+L\bar\tau)\).  Thus (12) controls that part directly.

The process \(A_k\) vanishes off \(\mathcal X_k\), and (2)--(4) bound its supremum norm by a fixed constant.  Therefore, (8) gives
\[
 E\|A_k-EA_k\|_\infty^2\le Ce^{-h_0k}\le Ck^{-2}.           \tag{20}
\]
Combining (10), (19), and (20) yields
\[
 E\|q_k^{\mathrm{PL}}\|_\infty^2\le C_0k^{-2}.             \tag{21}
\]
All constants in (8)--(21) depend only on the fixed overlap, hazard, and horizon bounds, so the estimates are uniform over \(\mathrm{def:exact-rank-class}\).

It remains to justify the donor averaging assertion without using a false Hilbert-space argument for the supremum norm.  For donor \(m\), write
\[
 U_m(t)=\int_0^tD_{m,k}(u)\,dM_m(u),
 \qquad V_{m,k}(t)=-S_m^0(t)U_m(t).
\]
The calculation in (17)--(18) gives
\[
 E\int_0^{\bar\tau}D_{m,k}(u)^2Y_m(u)\lambda_m^0(u)\,du
 \le Ck^{-2}                                                   \tag{22}
\]
uniformly in \(m\).  Integration by parts gives
\[
 \sum_{m\in\mathcal D}S_m^0(t)U_m(t)
 =\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}\int_0^tS_m^0(u)\,dU_m(u)
  +\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}\int_0^tU_m(u)\,dS_m^0(u).          \tag{23}
\]
The first term on the right of (23) is a martingale.  Independence of donor cells makes all cross predictable covariations zero, so (22) and the maximal inequality give its expected squared supremum at most \(CN_0k^{-2}\).  For the second term, use \(dS_m^0(u)=-S_m^0(u)\lambda_m^0(u)du\), Cauchy--Schwarz in \(u\), and independence together with \(EU_m(u)=0\):
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 E\sup_{t\in\mathcal I}
 \left|\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}\int_0^tU_m(u)\,dS_m^0(u)\right|^2
 &\le \bar\tau\int_0^{\bar\tau}
 E\left|\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}S_m^0(u)\lambda_m^0(u)U_m(u)\right|^2du\\
 &=\bar\tau\int_0^{\bar\tau}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}
 S_m^0(u)^2\lambda_m^0(u)^2EU_m(u)^2du\\
 &\le CN_0k^{-2}.
 \end{aligned}                                                 \tag{24}
\]
After division by \(N_0^2\), (23)--(24) prove
\[
 E\left\|N_0^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}V_{m,k}\right\|_\infty^2
 \le \frac{C}{N_0k^2}.                                      \tag{25}
\]

For completeness, the centered exhaustion processes obey the same donor rate.  Each \(A_{m,k}\) vanishes unless \(\mathcal X_{m,k}\) occurs, and differentiating (7) away from its single exhaustion time gives
\[
 A_{m,k}'(t)=-\lambda_m^0(t)A_{m,k}(t)
 +\widehat S_m^{\mathrm{PL}}(t)\{1-J_m(t)\}\lambda_m^0(t).
\]
Thus \(A_{m,k}(0)=0\) and
\[
 |A_{m,k}(t)-A_{m,k}(s)|
 \le C|t-s|\mathbf 1_{\mathcal X_{m,k}},
 \qquad
 \Pr(\mathcal X_{m,k})\le e^{-h_0k}.                        \tag{26}
\]
Here is a direct chaining bound.  After symmetrization by independent Rademacher signs \(\epsilon_m\), condition on the \(A_{m,k}\)'s and use dyadic partitions of \(\mathcal I\).  At level \(\ell\), there are at most \(2^{\ell+1}\) adjacent increments, and by (26) every signed increment is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy at most
\[
 C2^{-2\ell}N_0^{-2}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}
 \mathbf 1_{\mathcal X_{m,k}}.
\]
Exponential Markov's inequality and a union bound therefore bound the expected maximum at level \(\ell\) by a fixed multiple of
\[
 2^{-\ell}\sqrt{\ell+1}\,
 N_0^{-1}\left(\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}
 \mathbf 1_{\mathcal X_{m,k}}\right)^{1/2}.
\]
The sum over \(\ell\) is finite.  Integrating the same exponential tail gives the squared version, and ordinary ghost-sample symmetrization then yields
\[
 E\left\|N_0^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}
 \{A_{m,k}-EA_{m,k}\}\right\|_\infty^2
 \le \frac{C}{N_0^2}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}
 \Pr(\mathcal X_{m,k})
 \le \frac{Ce^{-h_0k}}{N_0}
 \le \frac{C}{N_0k^2}.                                      \tag{27}
\]
Combining (10), (25), and (27) proves
\[
 E\left\|N_0^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}q_{m,k}^{\mathrm{PL}}
 \right\|_\infty^2
 \le \frac{C}{N_0k^2}.                                      \tag{28}
\]

We now transfer the decomposition to the estimator actually constructed in \(\mathrm{def:fpcr-multiplier-band}\).  The linear interpolation operator \(\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}\) is deterministic, linear, and a contraction in supremum norm.  Define
\[
 \varphi_j=\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}\varphi_j^{\mathrm{PL}},
 \qquad b_k=\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}b_k^{\mathrm{PL}},
 \qquad q_k=\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}q_k^{\mathrm{PL}},
 \qquad a_{\mathcal G}=\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}S^0-S^0.
\]
Applying \(\mathcal L_{\mathcal G}\) to (6) proves
\[
 \widehat S^{\mathrm{KM}}-S^0
 =k^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^k\varphi_j+b_k+q_k+a_{\mathcal G}.        \tag{29}
\]
The operator preserves means and patient independence and cannot increase any of the bounds (11), (21), or (28).  It also commutes with donor averaging because all cells use the same deterministic grid.

Finally, the differential equation \((S^0)'=-\lambda^0S^0\) and \(\|\lambda^0\|_{C^\beta(\mathcal I)}\le L\) imply a uniform \(C^{\beta+1}\) bound on \(S^0\).  On each grid interval, write the interpolation error as the integral of differences of \((S^0)'\).  The \(C^{\min\{\beta,1\}}\) modulus of this derivative then gives
\[
 \|a_{\mathcal G}\|_\infty
 \le C_{\mathrm{int}}h_{N_0,K_T,K_D}^{q_\beta},
 \qquad q_\beta=\min\{\beta+1,2\}.                         \tag{30}
\]
This term cannot be absorbed into (11): for example, even without censoring, the chord between two grid values of a non-affine exponential survival curve has a nonzero interior interpolation error.

By (28) and Markov's inequality,
\[
 \left\|N_0^{-1}\sum_{m\in\mathcal D}q_{p,m,k}\right\|_\infty
 =O_P(N_0^{-1/2}k^{-1}).                                    \tag{31}
\]
Dividing the rate in (31) by \((N_0k)^{-1/2}\) gives \(k^{-1/2}\to0\).  The assumptions impose no cross-donor cancellation on the deterministic biases or interpolation errors, so their averages retain the corresponding uniform bounds.  Restoring cell subscripts proves all assertions.  For the deterministic odd--even split, the smaller fold has exactly \(\lfloor K_D/2\rfloor\) patients.  Substitution of this value for \(k\) in (11) gives, without replacing the floor by a looser half-sample bound,
\[
 C_0\exp\{-c_0\lfloor K_D/2\rfloor\},
\]
which is the stated contribution to \(\eta_D\).  This completes the proof. \(\square\)
