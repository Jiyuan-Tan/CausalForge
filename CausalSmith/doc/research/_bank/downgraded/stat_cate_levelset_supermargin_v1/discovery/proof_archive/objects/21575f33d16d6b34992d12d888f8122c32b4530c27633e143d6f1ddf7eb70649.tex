For \(a_0\le b_0\), define
\[
\begin{aligned}
 p_\lambda(a,y\mid x)
 &=\frac14+(y-\tfrac12)v_\lambda(x)
 +(2a-1)(2y-1)\frac{t_1(x)}4,\\
 q_\lambda(a,y\mid x)
 &=\frac14+(a-\tfrac12)u_\lambda(x)
 +(y-\tfrac12)\{v_\lambda(x)+t_0(x)u_\lambda(x)\}\\
 &\quad +(2a-1)(2y-1)
 \left\{\frac{t_0(x)}4+u_\lambda(x)v_\lambda(x)\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\]
For \(b_0\le a_0\), keep the same \(q_\lambda\) and instead set
\[
 p_\lambda(a,y\mid x)
 =\frac14+(a-\tfrac12)u_\lambda(x)
 +(y-\tfrac12)t_1(x)u_\lambda(x)
 +(2a-1)(2y-1)\frac{t_1(x)}4.
\]
All probabilities below are joint with the common density \(2^{-d}\) of \(X\). Direct Rademacher averaging and
\(
 t_1-t_0=4\mathbb E_\lambda(u_\lambda v_\lambda)
\)
give the exact equality of the one-observation mixtures
\[
 \bar p(a,y\mid x):=\mathbb E_\lambda p_\lambda(a,y\mid x)
 =\mathbb E_\lambda q_\lambda(a,y\mid x).
\]
Let \(\bar R\) be this common one-observation law. Positivity gives \(\bar p\ge\varepsilon\). Define centered likelihood perturbations
\[
 s_\lambda=\frac{p_\lambda-\bar p}{\bar p},
 \qquad
 r_\lambda=\frac{q_\lambda-\bar p}{\bar p}.
\]
They have mean zero in \(\lambda\) pointwise. Since \(u_\lambda\) and \(v_\lambda\) are linear Rademacher chaoses and \(u_\lambda v_\lambda-\mathbb E(u_\lambda v_\lambda)\) is a centered quadratic chaos, both \(s_\lambda(z)\) and \(r_\lambda(z)\) have finite Walsh expansions of degrees one and two.

We first record the coefficient comparison furnished by the displayed four-cell algebra. If \(a_0\le b_0\), the degree-one coefficients generated by \(v_\lambda\) are common to the two likelihoods and have magnitude at most \(Cb_0\). Every remaining degree-one coefficient has magnitude at most \(Ca_0\), and every remaining degree-two coefficient has magnitude at most \(Ca_0b_0\). If \(b_0\le a_0\), the common degree-one coefficients are those generated by \(u_\lambda\), with magnitude at most \(Ca_0\), while the residual degree-one and degree-two bounds are \(Cb_0\) and \(Ca_0b_0\). These assertions follow by subtracting the two displayed probabilities: their constant Walsh coefficients agree by the identity defining \(t_1-t_0\); the only quadratic term is the centered part of \(u_\lambda v_\lambda\). Division by \(\bar p\ge\varepsilon\) only changes the constant. Consequently, if
\[
 R=\max(a_0,b_0),
 \qquad D=\min(a_0,b_0),
\]
then common linear coefficients are bounded by \(CR\), residual linear coefficients by \(CD\), and residual quadratic coefficients by \(CDR\). At a fixed \(x\), at most a constant depending on \(D_0\) of these coefficients are nonzero.

Let
\[
 L_P(z_1,\ldots,z_n)=\mathbb E_\lambda\prod_{i=1}^n\{1+s_\lambda(z_i)\},
 \qquad
 L_Q(z_1,\ldots,z_n)=\mathbb E_\lambda\prod_{i=1}^n\{1+r_\lambda(z_i)\}.
\]
These are the two mixture likelihoods relative to \(\bar R^{\otimes n}\). We bound \(\|L_P-L_Q\|_{L_2(\bar R^{\otimes n})}\) by an explicit connected-component expansion. Expand every factor into its Walsh coefficients and then expand the square using two independent copies of the sign vector. A term is indexed by a bipartite incidence graph: observation vertices on the two copies are joined to the one or two sign labels in the selected Walsh monomial. Rademacher orthogonality makes the term zero unless every sign label has even degree within each sign copy. Components containing only common coefficients cancel between \(L_P\) and \(L_Q\).

Consider a surviving connected component and root it at the first residual coefficient in the lexicographic order of observation index, sign copy, and sign label. If the root is a residual linear coefficient, parity forces the same sign label to occur at another observation vertex. The product at this rooted pair is bounded either by \(CDR\), when the second occurrence is common, or by \(CD^2\), when it is residual. If the root is quadratic, its coefficient is already bounded by \(CDR\), and parity forces its labels to reappear. After squaring and integrating the two replicas, every rooted component therefore contains an anchor of weight at most
\[
 C\{D^2R^2+D^4\}
 =C\{a_0^2b_0^2+\min(a_0^4,b_0^4)\}.
\]
This is the promised anchor classification; it follows solely from Walsh parity and the coefficient bounds just proved.

There are at most \(K\) choices for the first anchor label. Each of the two anchor observation variables must lie in a fine support meeting that label, so the common uniform design and bounded overlap give an integrated spatial factor at most \(Cw^{2d}\). Once an anchor is fixed, order all remaining vertices in a spanning-tree traversal of their connected component. Every new vertex shares a sign label with an earlier vertex. There are at most \(C(D_0)\) choices for that label and support, at most \(n\) choices for its observation index, and its covariate integral is at most \(Cw^d\). Hence adding a vertex costs at most \(Cnw^d\). The sum over all possible additions is bounded by
\[
 \sum_{r=0}^\infty(Cnw^d)^r\le2.
\]
The ordering by the least unused vertex makes this an injection from connected incidence graphs into rooted traversals, so no uncounted factorial multiplicity remains. Thus the total weight of one connected component is bounded by \(CB\).

For several connected components, order them by their least observation vertex. The product expansion then gives the exponential series \(\sum_{m\ge1}(CB)^m/m!=e^{CB}-1\). If \(B\le b_0^*\) for a sufficiently small constant, this is at most \(C'B\). We have proved
\[
 \int(L_P-L_Q)^2\,d\bar R^{\otimes n}\le CB.
\]
Finally, Cauchy--Schwarz gives
\[
 \|\overline P_n-\overline Q_n\|_{\mathrm{TV}}^2
 \le\frac14\left\|L_P-L_Q\right\|_{L_1(\bar R^{\otimes n})}^2
 \le\frac14\left\|L_P-L_Q\right\|_{L_2(\bar R^{\otimes n})}^2
 \le CB.
\]
This proof uses the common uniform marginal explicitly and never divides by an \(n\)-sample mixture likelihood.
