\textbf{Proof.}
For \(\bar p\in[1/2,1)\), one has \(1\le2\bar p<2\), hence \(m_2=1\), \(2\in\mathcal N_{\bar p}\), and \(\Omega_2=\{10,01\}\).  No positive integer budget can satisfy \(1\le m\le n-1\) at \(n=1\), so this is the smallest nondegenerate exact-budget population.  Both displayed menus have two mappings.  Inspection of the rows at \(00\) and \(11\) in \(\mathrm{def{:}minimal\text{-}witness\text{-}mappings}\) shows that every unit's endpoint exposures are \(0\) and \(1\), hence distinct.

Write the endpoint vertices as \(a=(1,0)\), \(b=(1,1)\), \(c=(2,0)\), and \(d=(2,1)\).  Under \(e^{(0)}\), the endpoint-vertex sets realized at \(10\) and \(01\) are respectively \(\{a,c\}\) and \(\{a,d\}\).  Thus the nonedges are \(ac,ad\), and the edge set is \(\{ab,bc,bd,cd\}\).  Under \(e^{(-)}\), the two realized sets are \(\{a,c\}\) and \(\{b,c\}\); under \(e^{(+)}\), they occur in the reverse order.  Both latter graphs therefore have nonedges \(ac,bc\) and edge set \(\{ab,ad,bd,cd\}\).  This verifies the graph tables and the two identity isomorphisms in \(\mathrm{def{:}minimal\text{-}witness\text{-}graphs}\).

We next calculate the risks without treating any assertion in a construction definition as a proof.  For the lower-risk menu, the two schedules \(A\) and \(B\) tabulated in \(\mathrm{def{:}minimal\text{-}witness\text{-}arrays}\) both produce \((-1,1)\) at each feasible assignment, while their targets are \(1\) and \(-1\).  For an arbitrary estimator, let \(D_z\) denote its value on this common observation.  Under every design \(\pi\) on \(\Omega_2\),
\[
 \sum_{z\in\Omega_2}\pi_z
 \bigl[(D_z-1)^2+(D_z+1)^2\bigr]
 =2\sum_z\pi_zD_z^2+2\ge2. \tag{1}
\]
At least one schedule has mean squared error at least one.  Conversely, put all design mass on \(10\).  For either \(e^{(0)}\) or \(e^{(-)}\), write the bounded response coordinates as \((a,b,c,d)\in[-1,1]^4\).  The observation is \((a,c)\), the target is
\[
 t=\frac{b-a+d-c}{2},
\]
and \(\delta_-(10,(a,c))=-(a+c)/2\) has error
\[
 -\frac{a+c}{2}-t=-\frac{b+d}{2},
\]
whose absolute value is at most one.  Together with (1), this proves
\[
 V_2^\star(\mathcal E_-)=1. \tag{2}
\]

For \(\mathcal E_+\), consider the eight-atom prior specified in \(\mathrm{def{:}minimal\text{-}witness\text{-}arrays}\).  Its masses, with common denominator \(224\), are
\[
 30,30,35,35,5,5,42,42,
\]
which sum to \(224\).  Grouping the target moments by observed sign cell gives the following triples; each displayed triple is \(224\) times the cell mass, unnormalized target first moment, and unnormalized target second moment:
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
z& s &(224a_{zs},224b_{zs},224c_{zs})\\ \hline
10&(-1,1)&(112,-84,238)\\
10&(1,-1)&(112,84,238)\\ \hline
01&(-1,-1)&(72,54,198)\\
01&(1,1)&(72,-54,198)\\
01&(-1,1)&(40,30,40)\\
01&(1,-1)&(40,-30,40).
\end{array} \tag{3}
\]
These numbers follow by direct substitution into the two mapping tables: at \(10\), the observations are \((a,c)\) for \(e^{(0)}\) and \((b,c)\) for \(e^{(+)}\); at \(01\), they are \((a,d)\) and \((a,c)\), respectively.  Every mass in (3) is positive.  Therefore the posterior mean is \(b_{zs}/a_{zs}\), equal to \(-3s_2/4\) at \(10\) and \(-3s_1/4\) at \(01\).  The posterior-variance contributions \(c_{zs}-b_{zs}^2/a_{zs}\) are
\[
 \frac{25}{32},\ \frac{25}{32}
 \quad\text{at }10,
 \qquad
 \frac{45}{64},\ \frac{45}{64},\ \frac5{64},\ \frac5{64}
 \quad\text{at }01. \tag{4}
\]
They sum to \(25/16\) separately at either assignment.  The conditional square-loss identity
\[
 \mathbb E[(D-t)^2\mid z,s]
 =\operatorname{Var}(t\mid z,s)
  +\{D-\mathbb E(t\mid z,s)\}^2
\]
then shows that every estimator has Bayes mean squared error at least \(25/16\) under every design, including either boundary design.  Worst-case risk dominates this finite-prior average, so
\[
 V_2^\star(\mathcal E_+)\ge\frac54. \tag{5}
\]

For the reverse inequality, use probability \(1/2\) on each feasible assignment and the rule
\[
 \delta_+(10,x)=-\frac34x_2,
 \qquad
 \delta_+(01,x)=-\frac34x_1.
\]
For either mapping and a sign coordinate vector \((a,b,c,d)\in\{-1,1\}^4\), its mean squared error is
\[
 \frac12\left(-\frac34c-t\right)^2
 +\frac12\left(-\frac34a-t\right)^2,
 \qquad
 t=\frac{b-a+d-c}{2}. \tag{6}
\]
Enumerating the sixteen sign choices in (6) gives only the values
\(1/16\), \(9/16\), and \(25/16\); hence its maximum is \(25/16\).  The expression in (6) is convex in each one of \(a,b,c,d\) when the other three coordinates are fixed.  Successively maximizing a convex function over each interval \([-1,1]\) therefore moves every coordinate to an endpoint without decreasing the value.  Thus the same upper bound holds over the entire cube \([-1,1]^4\), for every bounded response array under either mapping.  Combining this bound with (5) gives
\[
 V_2^\star(\mathcal E_+)=\frac54. \tag{7}
\]
By \(\mathrm{thm{:}finite\text{-}saddle}\), (2) and (7) are also the exact finite-program frontiers.  Since the two mapping-wise labeled graph multisets agree but the risks differ, those graphs alone cannot represent latent-menu risk.  No assignment-level positivity, rank condition, or graph locality is used. \(\square\)
