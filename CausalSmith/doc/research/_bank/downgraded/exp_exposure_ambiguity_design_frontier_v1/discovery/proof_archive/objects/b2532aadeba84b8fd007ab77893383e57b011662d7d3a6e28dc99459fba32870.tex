\textbf{Proof.}
On the exact-budget slice, \(z\ne\mathbf 1_n\), so every \(e^{\mathrm{all}}\) sign schedule reveals its exposure-zero sign vector at every feasible assignment.  A constant schedule has target zero.  Two \(e^{\mathrm{all}}\) schedules that collide at one feasible assignment have the same exposure-zero vector, and hence collide at every feasible assignment; their targets can differ only through their exposure-one vectors, so their target separation is at most two.  Likewise, an \(e^{\mathrm{all}}\) schedule colliding with a constant schedule has the same exposure-zero vector as that constant schedule, and its target differs from zero by at most two.  Constant schedules have equal target zero.  Therefore every colliding pair has target separation at most two.

The bound is attained: take two \(e^{\mathrm{all}}\) sign schedules with the common exposure-zero response \(1\) at every unit, one with exposure-one response \(1\) everywhere and one with exposure-one response \(-1\) everywhere.  They collide under every \(z\in\Omega_n\), and their targets are \(0\) and \(-2\).  Thus for every probability vector \(\pi\),
\[
 C_n(\pi)=2. \tag{1}
\]

Fix any observation cell \((z,s)\) generated by a sign schedule.  Within the \(e^{\mathrm{all}}\) part of its fiber, hold the exposure-zero vector equal to \(s\) and choose the exposure-one vector freely in \(\{-1,1\}^n\).  The maximum and minimum targets differ by exactly
\[
 \frac1n\sum_{i\in V_n}\{1-(-1)\}=2. \tag{2}
\]
The preceding collision argument shows that no two targets in the full cross-model fiber can differ by more than two.  Hence every nonempty sign observation fiber has diameter two.  Consequently, for every \(\alpha\),
\[
 \sum_{z\in\Omega_n}\pi_zd_n(z,s_{\alpha z})^2=4,
\]
and therefore
\[
 U_n(\pi)=2. \tag{3}
\]
By \(\mathrm{prop{:}nonlocal\text{-}program\text{-}solution}\), \(V_n^\star=1\).  Substitution of (1)--(3) into \(\mathrm{thm{:}collision\text{-}geometry\text{-}sandwich}\) gives equality in both inequalities.  Thus neither universal factor \(1/2\) can be increased or decreased, respectively. \(\square\)
