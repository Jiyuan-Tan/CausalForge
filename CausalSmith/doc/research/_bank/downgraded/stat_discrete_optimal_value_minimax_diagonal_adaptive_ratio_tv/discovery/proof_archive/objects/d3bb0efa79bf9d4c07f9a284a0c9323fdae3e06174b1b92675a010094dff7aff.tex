\textbf{Proof.}
Write \(L_n=\log(en)\).  We first give the promised fuzzy mixture, without using the cited theorem.  Put \(k=2\lfloor d/2\rfloor\), let \(\mathbf Q\) be uniform on its first \(k\) coordinates, and, for \(\sigma\in\{-1,1\}^{k/2}\), define
\[
 P_{\sigma,2j-1}=\frac{1+\alpha\sigma_j}{k},
 \qquad
 P_{\sigma,2j}=\frac{1-\alpha\sigma_j}{k},
 \qquad
 \alpha^2=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{16n}.
\]
If \(d\leq n^2\), then \(0<\alpha\leq1/4\), so these are probability vectors in \(\Delta_d\).  If \(L_\sigma=d\mathbf P_\sigma^n/d\mathbf Q^n\), independence and a direct sum over each coordinate pair give
\[
 E_{\mathbf Q^n}(L_\sigma L_\tau)
 =\left(1+\frac{2\alpha^2}{k}
       \sum_{j=1}^{k/2}\sigma_j\tau_j\right)^n.
\]
Let \(M=2^{-k/2}\sum_\sigma\mathbf P_\sigma^n\).  The inequalities \(1+u\leq e^u\) and \(\cosh u\leq e^{u^2/2}\) imply
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 1+\chi^2(M,\mathbf Q^n)
 &=2^{-k}\sum_{\sigma,\tau}E_{\mathbf Q^n}(L_\sigma L_\tau)\\
 &\leq E\exp\!\left\{
       \frac{2n\alpha^2}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k/2}Z_j
      \right\}\\
 &=\left\{\cosh\!\left(\frac{2n\alpha^2}{k}\right)\right\}^{k/2}
 \leq\exp\!\left(\frac{n^2\alpha^4}{k}\right)
 =e^{1/256},
 \end{aligned}
\]
where the \(Z_j\)'s are independent Rademacher variables.  Moreover,
\[
 \lVert\mathbf P_\sigma-\mathbf Q\rVert_1=\alpha
 \quad\text{for every }\sigma.
\]
In the two-stream experiment the second stream has law \(\mathbf Q^n\) under the null and every alternative, and hence cancels from the likelihood ratio.  Since total variation is at most the square root of chi-square divergence,
\[
 \lVert M-\mathbf Q^n\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}
 \leq\sqrt{e^{1/256}-1}<\frac12.
\]
For any estimator \(T\), use the event \(E=\{T\geq\alpha/2\}\).  Its risk under the null target zero is at least \(\alpha^2P_{\mathbf Q^n}(E)/4\), and its risk averaged over the alternatives, all of whose targets equal \(\alpha\), is at least \(\alpha^2M(E^c)/4\).  Therefore
\[
 \sup_{(\mathbf P,\mathbf Q)\in\Delta_d^2}
 E[(T-\lVert\mathbf P-\mathbf Q\rVert_1)^2]
 \geq\frac{\alpha^2}{8}
       \{1-\lVert M-\mathbf Q^n\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\}
 \geq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{256n}.
 \tag{1}
\]
Taking the infimum over \(T\) proves (1) for \(\mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}\).

Choose \(\rho\leq1\).  Since \(L_n\leq n\), the range \(d\leq\rho nL_n\) lies inside \(d\leq n^2\), so (1) is available throughout that range.  Also \(k\geq d/2\).  Consequently, if \(d\leq D L_n^2\), then
\[
 \mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}
 \geq\frac{\sqrt d}{256\sqrt2,n}
 \geq\frac{1}{256\sqrt{2D}}
       \frac{d}{nL_n}.
 \tag{2}
\]

It remains to cover the upper band.  Fix \(C=1/2\) in \(\mathrm{lem:jiao-han-weissman-known-q-lower}\), and let \(C'>0\) be its associated dominance constant.  For all sufficiently large \(n\), every \(d\leq\rho nL_n\) satisfies \(\log n\geq(1/2)\log d\).  Shrink \(\rho\), if necessary, so that \(d\leq n\log n\) in this range.  For the uniform vector \(Q_x=1/d\),
\[
 T_{n,d}(\mathbf Q)
 =d\sqrt{\frac{1/d}{n\log n}}
 =\sqrt{\frac{d}{n\log n}}.
 \tag{3}
\]
The first term in the cited dominance condition is bounded by (3) whenever
\(d\geq4(C')^2(\log n)^2\).  The second is bounded by (3) whenever
\(n\geq(C')^2(\log n)^3\).  Thus, after choosing \(D\) sufficiently large, the cited theorem and its fixed-multinomial correction give
\[
 \inf_T\sup_{\mathbf P\in\Delta_d}
 E_{\mathbf P}\!left[
  \{T-\lVert\mathbf P-\mathbf Q\rVert_1\}^2
 \right]
 \geq c_0\frac{d}{n\log n}
 \tag{4}
\]
throughout the upper band, up to numerical changes in sample size and constants allowed by that correction.  A two-stream estimator can be used in this known-\(\mathbf Q\) experiment by generating an independent \(n\)-sample from the known vector \(\mathbf Q\).  The known-\(\mathbf Q\) experiment is therefore at least as informative, and its minimax risk in (4) is no larger than \(\mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}\).  Since \(\log n\) and \(L_n\) are comparable for large \(n\), (4) proves the desired upper-band bound.

Finally, choose \(\rho>0\) small enough that the upper band is empty for the finitely many \(n\) preceding all of the preceding large-\(n\) conditions.  Equations (2) and (4) then cover every \(n\geq1\) and every \(2\leq d\leq\rho nL_n\).  Taking the smaller of their numerical constants completes the proof.  Notice that no overlap or causal assumption is used in this purely multinomial lemma.
