\textbf{Proof.}
Because \(d\leq n^2\), one has \(k\leq n^2\) and therefore
\(\alpha^2=\sqrt{k}/(16n)\leq1/16\).  Thus \(0<\alpha\leq1/4\), every displayed coordinate is nonnegative, each pair has mass \(2/k\), and \(\mathbf P_\sigma,\mathbf Q\in\Delta_d\).  Directly,
\[
 \lVert\mathbf P_\sigma-\mathbf Q\rVert_1
 =\sum_{j=1}^{k/2}\left(\frac\alpha k+\frac\alpha k\right)
 =\alpha.
 \tag{1}
\]

Let \(L_\sigma=d\mathbf P_\sigma^n/d\mathbf Q^n\) and let
\(M=2^{-k/2}\sum_\sigma\mathbf P_\sigma^n\).  For two sign vectors,
\[
 E_{\mathbf Q^n}(L_\sigma L_\tau)
 =\left\{
   \sum_{x=1}^k\frac{P_{\sigma,x}P_{\tau,x}}{Q_x}
  \right\}^n
 =\left(1+\frac{2\alpha^2}{k}
       \sum_{j=1}^{k/2}\sigma_j\tau_j\right)^n.
 \tag{2}
\]
The base in (2) is nonnegative because it is a sum of nonnegative terms.  Averaging (2), applying \(1+u\leq e^u\), and writing the products \(\sigma_j\tau_j\) as independent Rademacher variables gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 1+\chi^2(M,\mathbf Q^n)
 &\leq\left\{\cosh\!\left(
       \frac{2n\alpha^2}{k}
      \right)\right\}^{k/2}\\
 &\leq\exp\!\left(\frac{n^2\alpha^4}{k}\right)
 =e^{1/256},
 \end{aligned}
 \tag{3}
\]
where \(\cosh u\leq e^{u^2/2}\) was used in the second line.  This proves the stated chi-square bound.

In the two-multinomial experiment the second sample has law \(\mathbf Q^n\) under the null and under every alternative, so it does not change (3).  Moreover,
\[
 \lVert M-\mathbf Q^n\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}
 \leq\sqrt{\chi^2(M,\mathbf Q^n)}
 \leq\sqrt{e^{1/256}-1}<\frac12.
 \tag{4}
\]
For any estimator \(T\), on \(E=\{T\geq\alpha/2\}\) its squared error under the null target zero is at least \(\alpha^2/4\), whereas on \(E^c\) its squared error under every alternative target \(\alpha\) is at least \(\alpha^2/4\).  Therefore
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \sup_{(\mathbf P,\mathbf Q)\in\Delta_d^2}
 E[(T-\lVert\mathbf P-\mathbf Q\rVert_1)^2]
 &\geq\frac{\alpha^2}{8}
   \{P_{\mathbf Q^n}(E)+M(E^c)\}\\
 &\geq\frac{\alpha^2}{8}
   \{1-\lVert M-\mathbf Q^n\rVert_{\mathrm{TV}}\}\\
 &\geq\frac{\alpha^2}{16}
 =\frac{\sqrt{k}}{256n}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Taking the infimum over \(T\) proves the first risk inequality.  By
\(\mathrm{lem:l1-lower-transfer}\),
\[
 \mathfrak R_{n,d,\epsilon}
 \geq\frac1{16}\mathfrak R^{L_1}_{n,d}
 \geq\frac{\sqrt{k}}{4096n}.
\]
Finally, \(k=2\lfloor d/2\rfloor\geq d/2\) for \(d\geq2\), which gives the stated causal lower bound.  All zero coordinates are legitimate simplex boundary points, and the causal embedding has propensity \(1/2\), satisfying fixed overlap for every \(0<\epsilon<1/2\).
