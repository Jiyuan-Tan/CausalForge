\textbf{Proof.}
Each local vector \(v_k\) has two entries equal to \(1\), two equal to \(-1\), and squared norm \(4\).  Hence every \(s\in\mathscr S\) is balanced in each quadruple, so \(s\in\mathcal H\), and
\[
\operatorname{tr}(ss^\top)=\|s\|_2^2=4q=n.
\tag{1}
\]
The real vector space of symmetric operators on the \(d\)-dimensional space \(\mathcal H\) has dimension \(D=d(d+1)/2\).  All atoms \(ss^\top\) lie in the affine trace hyperplane
\[
\{A\in\mathbb S(\mathcal H):\operatorname{tr}(A)=n\},
\tag{2}
\]
whose affine dimension is at most \(D-1\).

Let \(Q^\star=\sum_s\alpha_s^star ss^\top\) be the sign-pair representation of an optimizer furnished by \(\mathrm{thm:higher\text{-}rank\text{-}menu\text{-}exact\text{-}program}\).  If more than \(D\) coefficients are positive, the corresponding atoms are affinely dependent in (2).  Thus there are real numbers \(c_s\), not all zero and supported on the positive coefficients, such that
\[
\sum_sc_s=0,
\qquad
\sum_sc_sss^\top=0.
\tag{3}
\]
After replacing \(c\) by \(-c\) if necessary, at least one \(c_s>0\).  Put
\[
\varepsilon=\min_{s:c_s>0}\frac{\alpha_s^star}{c_s}>0,
\qquad
\alpha'_s=\alpha_s^star-\varepsilon c_s.
\tag{4}
\]
Equations (3)--(4) preserve nonnegativity, total mass one, and the second moment, while at least one formerly positive coefficient becomes zero.  Iterating this finite elimination gives a mass vector \(\widetilde\alpha\) with at most \(D\) positive entries and
\[
\sum_s\widetilde\alpha_s ss^\top=Q^\star.
\tag{5}
\]
There were only \(m=6^q/2\) sign pairs to begin with, so the support bound is \(\min\{m,D\}\).

Define the blinded law by \(\widetilde\pi(s)=\widetilde\pi(-s)=\widetilde\alpha_s/2\).  It belongs to \(\Pi_n\), and (5) gives \(Q_{\widetilde\pi}=Q^\star\).  Every generalized class risk and every constraint of the exact program depends on the law only through this second moment, so all risks and \(\lambda_{\mathrm{gen}}^\star\) are unchanged.  Blinding uses at most twice the number of positive sign-pair masses.  The elimination proves existence after \(Q^\star\) is known; it neither finds \(Q^\star\) nor avoids the exponential-column program.  \(\square\)
